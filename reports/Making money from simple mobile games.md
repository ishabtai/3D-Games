# Build Mr. Not Fair a small, honest kids' game

**Bottom line:** For "some small cash" from the Mr. Not Fair IP, build one small, ad-free game for ages 4-7. Give it a free taste and a one-time parent-approved unlock. Sell it as a companion to the book to the readers, parents and teachers you already reach. Expect **hundreds to low thousands of dollars**, not a living. Don't chase a Block Blast or Candy Crush-style hit. Mechanics are legal to copy, but the money in that segment goes to studios that spend millions on ads and testing. A solo game with no marketing budget usually earns **$0 to $100 in total** ([Sensor Tower via GameDevReports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/sensor-tower-1-of-top-gaming-companies); [QuickBits devlog](https://quickbits.itch.io/guess-the-crypto/devlog/294463/how-much-has-our-first-mobile-app-generated-so-far)). Kids' games earn less per player and come with strict privacy rules. They still suit you better, because your character is a picture-book character, which makes the game legally "for children" whatever you call it, and because your book audience is your only real marketing channel. Cash costs are small, about **$124 in store fees** plus access to a Mac. Plan on **2-8 weeks of building plus 3-6 weeks of store paperwork**. If you later want ad income, make it a separate, non-childlike puzzle game and put it on web portals first.

## The money is real but sits with the top 1%

Mobile puzzle games are huge: **over $10B in in-app purchases in 2025** ([Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/us-mobile-puzzle-4-point-6-billion-revenue)). Almost none of it reaches small developers. The top 1% of publishers took **92.5% of game IAP revenue** in 2025 ([GameDevReports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/sensor-tower-1-of-top-gaming-companies)). Only about **10% of new apps get any user attention at all** ([MobileAction](https://www.mobileaction.co/blog/organic-app-growth-in-2025/)). Among subscription apps, the median earns **under $50/month** after a year ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026)). One developer's first ad-supported game earned **$0.07** in its first month from 21 downloads ([QuickBits](https://quickbits.itch.io/guess-the-crypto/devlog/294463/how-much-has-our-first-mobile-app-generated-so-far)).

Each way of charging has a typical price and a typical player base:

| Model | Typical numbers | Who it works for |
|---|---|---|
| Ads (rewarded video, interstitials) | US rewarded video ~$15-20 per 1,000 views ([Tenjin](https://tenjin.com/blog/ad-mon-gaming-2026/)); simple ad games earn ~$0.03-0.08 per daily player ([Game Growth Advisor](https://gamegrowthadvisor.com/blog/2026-03-17-mobile-game-kpis-benchmarks-2026/)) | General-audience games with thousands of daily players |
| In-app purchases | Only 1.5-5% of players ever pay ([MAF](https://maf.ad/en/blog/mobile-game-conversion-rates/)) | Games with deep content and live updates (Royal Match: $1.4B in 2025, no ads) |
| Subscription | Kids leaders earn $0.5-3M/month (Lingokids ~$3M/month est.) ([Sensor Tower](https://app.sensortower.com/overview/1002043426?country=US)) | Brands with a large content library and paid marketing |
| Premium / one-time unlock | Only ~4% of downloads are paid, but premium releases rose 77% in 2025 ([daily.dev](https://daily.dev/posts/premium-mobile-games-are-back-with-releases-up-77-in-2025-mo3xb5ehx)) | Niche games with a known audience, including kids' games |

Two facts favour small developers. Both stores take only **15% on your first $1M**; Apple makes you sign up for this rate and Google applies it automatically ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/engineering/small-business-program)). Ad revenue carries no store commission at all. The catch with ads is scale. Block Blast reached about **70M daily players** yet earns only about 1-2 cents per player per day ([Udonis](https://www.blog.udonis.co/statistics/block-blast)). At that rate, even 1,000 daily players brings in only about $15-80/day. Flippa listings for small ad games that happen to have about 550 active installs show **$89-188/month** ([Flippa](https://flippa.com/10548430-swipe-it)).

## Buying players loses money, and publishers accept almost nobody

On iOS, puzzle game installs cost roughly **$1.40-3.00 each**, and that price is still rising ([Liftoff](https://liftoff.ai/2025-casual-gaming-apps-report/); [Mapendo](https://mapendo.co/blog/mobile-games-cpi-2025)). A simple ad-funded game earns a few cents a day per player and keeps under 4% of players after a week ([GameAnalytics](https://www.gameanalytics.com/reports/2026-mobile-pc-gaming-benchmarks)), so paid ads almost never pay for themselves for a solo developer. That leaves free channels. A short TikTok or Shorts video can take off, but that is a lottery ticket ([presskit.gg](https://presskit.gg/field-guides/tiktok-indie-game-marketing)). Otherwise you need store featuring, or an audience you already have.

Publishers such as Voodoo, Homa, Kwalee and SayGames will pay to test your prototype's ad costs for free, but they reject almost everything. Voodoo tests about 1,000 prototypes a year and fully launches **about 0.4%** of them ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/06/02/how-voodoo-builds-hits-four-product-lessons-from-a-2000-prototypes-a-year-machine)). A prototype typically needs installs under **$0.30-0.50** and roughly **35-40% of players returning the next day** before a publisher will scale it ([Gamesforum report](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Gamesforum-Intelligence-Hypercasual-Gaming-Report.pdf)). Revenue-share terms are private. Treat a publisher test as a free validation experiment, not a plan. A kids' game does not fit these publishers anyway.

## Copying Block Blast is legal but commercially pointless

The law is clear enough. In the US, **you can copy game rules** ("drag pieces onto a grid, clear lines") but **not the look**: piece style, colours, art, name or icon. In 2012 a court ruled against an iPhone Tetris clone because it copied Tetris's visual expression "akin to literal copying" ([Tetris v. Xio opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115/pdf/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115-0.pdf)). The store rules are stricter than the law. Apple rejects apps that "simply copy the latest popular app" or are "indistinguishable from what's already widely available" (guidelines 4.1 and 4.3) ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)).

The bigger problem is crowding. **Over 1,000 new block puzzles launched in the first half of 2025 alone** ([AppMagic](https://appmagic.rocks/research/casual-report-h1-2025)). The games that overtook older leaders won by out-executing and out-spending them. Block Blast relied on constant A/B testing and heavy ad buying ([Mobilegamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/block-blast-maker-hungry-studio-a-b-tested-its-way-to-the-top-of-the-charts/)). Royal Match got **61.5% of its installs from paid ads**, backed by a lovable original character ([Naavik](https://naavik.co/deep-dives/royal-match/)). The lesson for you is the character, not the mechanic. A familiar mechanic plus Mr. Not Fair gives you something that stands out. A bare clone does not.

## Kids' games pay less per player but fit your IP

A game for ages 4-7 falls under strict privacy rules, and the practical upshot is simple: **collect nothing**.

- **US law (COPPA):** the updated rules have applied since **April 22, 2026**. You cannot use targeted ads or share children's data with ad networks without separate, verified parental consent. Fines reach **$53,088 per violation** ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)).
- **Apple:** games in the Kids category may not include third-party ads or analytics, apart from narrow exceptions. Purchases and links must sit behind a **parental gate**, such as an adult-only puzzle ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)).
- **Google Play:** child-audience apps may use only certified ad SDKs ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12955712)). The Kids tab shows only **Teacher Approved** apps, and teachers mark games down for ad and purchase pressure ([Android Developers Blog](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2022/11/helping-kids-and-families-find-high-quality-apps-for-kids.html)).

Ads in kids' games earn **2.5-5 times less** than in general games ([GameBiz Consulting](https://www.gamebizconsulting.com/newsletter/admon-newsletter-11-the-shrinking-island-ad-monetization-underage-users)). That rules out ads for you. The big earners, such as Lingokids, Sago Mini and Pok Pok, use subscriptions, and those need a large content library plus paid marketing ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/whats-the-best-way-to-monetize-kids-apps/)). A famous brand does sell: the World of Peppa Pig app earns about **$500K/month** ([Sensor Tower](https://app.sensortower.com/ios/us/entertainment-one/app/world-of-peppa-pig/1175384784)). But Peppa has TV-level fame behind it, and a book-only character does not.

**Kids vs. general casual, as a money bet:** general casual games have the higher theoretical ceiling, but they are a hits business that depends on paid ads. Putting a picture-book character in one does not escape the kids' rules either. US law now counts your marketing, your reviews and your character's style as evidence of who the game is for ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/05/childrens-online-privacy-in-2025-the-amended-coppa-rule)). So a Mr. Not Fair game *is* a kids' game. The kids' route has a lower ceiling but also less competition. It gets you teacher and parent word of mouth, and the game sells the book while the book sells the game. It is the better bet for you.

## Costs are tiny; time and paperwork are the real price

| Item | Reality |
|---|---|
| Store fees | Apple $99/year; Google $25 once ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/programs/enroll/); [Google](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/6112435)) |
| Engine | Godot free; GameMaker $99.99 one-time; Unity free under $200K revenue ([GameMaker](https://gamemaker.io/en/get); [Unity](https://unity.com/products/pricing-updates)) |
| Mac | Required for iOS builds (Xcode 26) ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ueeok6yw)); alternatively use a cloud Mac or a build service |
| Google hurdle | New personal accounts must run a **14-day test with 12 testers** before release ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465?hl=en)) |
| Build time | 2-8 weeks for a simple game; one developer built a Suika clone in under 2 weeks with ChatGPT and earned ~$10K, mostly from web portals ([Medium](https://medium.com/@anulagarwal12/i-built-a-game-using-chatgpt-in-2-weeks-that-made-10-000-1e766374749f)) |
| Web portals (general-audience games only) | Poki splits revenue 50/50; good casual games earn ~$200-2,000/month ([Playgama](https://playgama.com/blog/business-faqs/poki-vs-crazygames-vs-gamedistribution-revenue-share/); [Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/web-game-monetization)) |

## Recommended path

1. **Build one small game (4-6 weeks).** Pick a simple, calm mechanic such as matching, sorting or sharing puzzles, built around the book's fairness theme. Use Godot or GameMaker, and use AI tools to speed up the work.
2. **Ship with zero ads and zero tracking.** The first few levels are free. A one-time unlock of roughly $2.99-4.99 sits behind a parental gate. This price is a suggestion, not a benchmark from the research.
3. **Sell through the audience you already have.** Put a QR code in the book, show the game at readings and school visits, and pitch it to teachers as a social-emotional learning tool. Apply for Google's Teacher Approved programme, and make "no ads, no data" part of your pitch to parents.
4. **Keep costs close to $124**, set aside 3-6 weeks for store setup, and judge the game on book-plus-app income, not on app sales alone.
5. **Optional second bet.** If you want ad money, build a separate, non-childlike puzzle game for web portals first and wrap it for the app stores later.

## Conclusion

The real choice is not ads versus purchases. It is **distribution you own versus distribution you have to buy**. You can't buy players profitably, and copying a hit's mechanic gives you no edge over 1,000 competitors. The one thing you have that a clone-maker doesn't is **a character with readers attached**. That makes the game worth most as part of a small book-and-game product line. On its own it would be a very long lottery ticket.

A few things are uncertain. There is no public data on typical earnings for a solo kids' game, and several figures here are secondary estimates. Get a short legal check of your privacy setup before launch. The most useful early test costs nothing: see whether your existing readers actually download the free version.
